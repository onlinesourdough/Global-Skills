# Shape Offer

Shape one useful offer hypothesis from the context that is actually available.
The output is a concise Offer Brief and a smallest next validation, not a
campaign, funnel, product build, or promise of business growth.

## Keep the boundary

- Optimize for fit, useful outcomes, sustainable delivery, and an informed
  customer choice. Do not optimize for maximum volume, automation, or
  conversion at any cost.
- Do not add direct-response funnels, bonuses, invented scarcity or urgency,
  unsupported guarantees, inflated outcomes, or claims that the evidence does
  not support.
- Do not turn a founder belief, an aspiration, or a plausible mechanism into
  customer evidence. If evidence is absent, say that it is absent.
- Treat delivery capacity, energy, attention, cash, time, privacy, and other
  owner constraints as design inputs. A smaller offer that can be delivered
  well is a valid recommendation.
- Stop at the brief and validation. Do not build the product, launch a
  campaign, contact prospects, set up payments, or change operations.

## Build an evidence ledger first

Use only the supplied context and clearly identified observations. Classify
each material item before shaping the offer:

- **Known fact:** directly supplied, observed, or linked to a named source.
  Preserve the source or date when one is provided.
- **Assumption:** a temporary, reversible interpretation needed to make
  progress. State it plainly and keep it from carrying a material decision.
- **Unknown:** missing, conflicting, or unverified information that could
  change the customer, problem, outcome, scope, price, delivery model, or
  boundary.

Never fill an unknown with a statistic, testimonial, market size, competitor
claim, price benchmark, conversion rate, or invented customer language.

## Shape the offer

Address these inputs as relevant to the decision, using the evidence ledger:

1. **Customer and real problem.** Name the specific person or situation the
   offer is for and the problem they are trying to solve. Separate the stated
   request from the underlying problem only when the context supports that
   distinction. Mark the problem as a hypothesis when it is not evidenced.
2. **Desired outcome and evidence.** State the observable change the customer
   wants and what would count as progress. Record existing evidence such as a
   paid result, repeated use, a concrete artifact, or a direct observation
   only when it is supplied. Do not promise the outcome merely because the
   method seems capable of producing it.
3. **Scope and delivery.** Specify what is included, format, duration or
   cadence, support and response boundaries, customer responsibilities, and
   explicit exclusions. If any of these are unset, leave them as unknowns
   rather than silently expanding the offer.
4. **Price logic and economics.** Record the price or range only if supplied.
   Explain the available logic using the actual inputs: customer value,
   alternatives, delivery time, direct costs, owner compensation, capacity,
   risk, and payment or refund terms. If the inputs are missing, describe the
   decision that remains; do not manufacture a price or margin.
5. **Owner burden and constraints.** Account for weekly time, energy,
   availability, emotional load, administration, handoffs, maximum comfortable
   capacity, and any stated business boundaries. Prefer the simplest delivery
   model that can meet the proposed scope without quietly shifting burden to
   the owner.
6. **Boundaries and unsupported promises.** List prerequisites, exclusions,
   risks, and what the offer does not promise. Replace certainty with a
   conditional statement when the evidence is limited. Do not use guarantees,
   urgency, scarcity, bonuses, or pressure as substitutes for fit.
7. **Decision and smallest validation.** Recommend one offer shape when the
   context supports a choice. If two shapes remain viable, name the tradeoff
   and choose the more reversible one. Design one small validation that tests
   the most material uncertainty before more scope or infrastructure is
   added.

## Handle incomplete context

Use the smallest reversible assumption only when it cannot materially change
the offer. Label it in the brief and state what would invalidate it.

When an unknown could materially change the offer, do not ask a long intake
questionnaire. Identify the single material question, explain why it matters,
and either ask for that answer or make it the subject of the smallest next
validation. If context is contradictory, preserve the contradiction and stop
at `needs-one-material-decision`.

The smallest validation should include:

- one customer/problem hypothesis;
- one reversible action, such as a conversation, invitation, or paid pilot,
  sized to the stated constraints;
- one observable signal that would support or weaken the hypothesis;
- a time, effort, or participant boundary using supplied numbers where
  available; and
- a learn/stop rule that says what changes after the signal.

Do not turn the validation into a launch plan, audience-building system, or
full product build. If no credible validation can be proposed from the
context, name the missing material input instead.

## Return this Offer Brief

Return a concise Offer Brief with the offer hypothesis, decision and rationale,
the seven input areas above, and a distinguishable ledger of known facts,
reversible assumptions, and unknowns. Include the Smallest next validation
with its material question, action, signal, boundary, and learn/stop rule.

Use `decision-ready` only when no unresolved unknown can materially change
the offer. Otherwise use `needs-one-material-decision`, identify that one
frontier question or validation, and do not imply that the offer is proven.
